# Ideation Jam & Inspiration Log Pt. 3
ATLAS 4000 · Kaitlyn Samuelian

## What idea / diagram / topic / etc. are you focusing on? Did this change from last week?

I am focusing on a drying system that actually helps clothes dry (airflow, rod geometry, materials, how garments sit) and that someone can live around, not just hide.

Last week I was mostly looking at modular racks and renter-friendly install: floor vs wall vs door, and whether the frame should change shape for pants vs sports bras. After talking with Aidan I am not sure people want to reconfigure a rack every load. That feels like adding a chore to a chore. So this week I looked at what expensive or engineered products do that a cheap folding rack does not: move air, heat the bars, enclose the load, move the hanger, or look like they belong in a room. Cost is not my first constraint right now. I want the strongest capstone. Affordable vs premium can be decided later.

## PART TWO. Continuing to find patterns

### Q1. When you look through your Inspiration Log for this week and the previous two weeks, do you see similar patterns? Any new ones? What patterns do you see?

Yes. Some patterns showed up when I was looking at normal racks and they are still true. Some only showed up once I started looking at systems that try to dry faster, or that are more designed as objects.

Still true from earlier weeks:
- If you want a lot of drying space, you usually have to screw into a wall. If you cannot drill, you usually end up hanging something on a door, or living with a floor rack. Stick-on hardware is for pictures, not a wet load.
- Most products only live one way in a room. Door, wall, or floor. If the next place is different, people buy something else.
- The rack is in the way for a long time. People hang laundry and then live around it. Folding it away after it is installed is something people like. A rack that stays ugly in the living room is a real complaint.
- People already sort a mixed load on a fixed frame (light stuff lower, heavier stuff higher, long things on the ends). I have not seen anyone change the frame itself between loads. The rearranging they hate is flipping damp stuff while it is drying.

New from this week, plus more research into existing systems:
- While I was doing more research into existing systems, I kept seeing the same split. Some products heat the bars. Some blow air into the space around the clothes. Some zip or cabinet the load in so the air cannot escape. A few move the hanger. Almost none of the things you can actually buy push air out through the bars the clothes sit on.
- The failure people describe is unevenness: the part not touching the bar, the folded bit, the item in the back. Heat-only racks still have that problem, which is why I put one in this week’s log.
- Things that dry faster often look like appliances or camping gear. Things that look like they belong in a room often do nothing extra to help drying. The gap I care about is in the middle: actually helps, still okay to have out.
- On expensive garment-care systems, motion, spacing, and enclosure are designed. On a cheap rack the user improvises all three in ten seconds with wet hands. The Styler in this week’s log is the clearest example of that.

### Q2. What about what you are proposing is similar / different from what’s been done before?

**Similar:**
I am still in the “hang wet clothes in the home instead of a tumble dryer” category. Same job as every drying rack. I still care about small spaces and not wrecking a rental, because that is my actual life. A plug-in object you hang on the same way as a normal rack (heated bars, or my airflow idea) is the same habit. Folding away or getting it off the floor is already a thing products try to do.

**Different:**
I am not proposing another cheap floor rack, and I am not proposing a box of extra mounts. Aidan made me doubt reconfiguration as the main feature. What I want to test is whether the structure can make drying faster and more even without asking someone to rebuild the rack every load.

While I was doing more research into existing systems, that gap got clearer. People already pay for racks that do something (heat, a fan, a bag around the load). Garment-care closets design how clothes sit in the air, but they are not for a real mixed wash. I have not found a mixed-load rack you live with that pushes air through the bars themselves. Enclosing just enough air (not a huge closet, not a zip tent) is another open question. Looking like it can stay in the room, instead of looking temporary or like camping gear, is a third. None of those are combined in the cheap folding rack, and the expensive closets do not take a full load of laundry.

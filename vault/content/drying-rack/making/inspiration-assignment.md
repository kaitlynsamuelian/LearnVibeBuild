# Inspiration / precedent assignment
ATLAS 4000 · 30 September 2026 · Kaitlyn Samuelian

## What idea / diagram / topic / etc. are you focusing on? Did this change from last week?

I am focusing on how a drying system can actively improve air-drying through airflow, materials, geometry, or integrated mechanisms, while still fitting into someone’s living space.

Yes, this changed from last week. Last week I was looking at modular drying-rack configurations and renter-friendly installation (how it mounts, whether the frame should change shape for pants vs sports bras). After talking with Aidan Chopra, I am questioning whether people would actually want to spend time reconfiguring a rack for every load. So this week I am looking at other ways the product could make drying better without adding a chore.

I am especially interested in airflow, rod geometry, materials, garment spacing, and systems where the structure itself helps clothes dry. I am not constraining the project around cost yet. Affordable is still possible later. Right now I want the strongest capstone, even if that leads toward a more premium or technically complex product.

These six examples are new pieces of inspiration, on top of last week’s rack precedents (Polder, WallFix, SKÅDIS, and the install-family research). They tell a story: cheap open rack → active drying → airflow built into an object → how garments sit in the air → air through a hollow structure → living with it in a room.

## #1. Dyson Air Multiplier (bladeless fan)

Suggested screenshot: Dyson Cool / Air Multiplier product photo, or Fig. 1 from US patent 8,092,166 (the ring nozzle on a base).
https://patents.google.com/patent/US8092166B2/en

**Link / source:**
US Patent 8,092,166 B2, “Fan,” Dyson Technology Ltd, granted 2012.
https://patents.google.com/patent/US8092166B2/en
Related parametric study: Maîtrejean et al., “Parametric Study of a Bladeless Fan Geometry,” 2024.
https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03305
Dyson has not made a drying rack. This is design and airflow logic only.

**Brief description:**
The moving parts sit in the base. The visible object is a ring that shapes the air (a narrow annular slot, patent range 0.5 mm to 10 mm) and pulls in surrounding room air. A 2024 study found slit thickness changed performance more than other geometry variables they tested.

**Reflection:**
I like that the air path is the product, not a grille over a fan. A cheap drying rack has no air path at all. I would not copy Dyson’s motor or price. I would steal putting the fan in a foot, making the exit a thin slot, and treating the living-room object as calm furniture rather than an appliance.

**Brief description:**
A closed clothing-care cabinet. Official wording on the page we opened: a Jet Air system and Air Hangers “blow powerful jets of air upwards and downwards,” plus “low temperature drying using an upper JetAir and a lower HeatPump” to “dry clothes evenly.” Capacity on that page is three jackets plus three pairs of pants. **Reflection:**
I like that Samsung names evenness, not just speed, and attacks the garment from more than one direction. I do not like the scale: 89 kg, wardrobe-sized, about $1,600, and it cannot take towels, socks, or bras from a real mixed load. The principle to steal is enclose the air, then deliver it through the thing the clothes hang on. I do not need to build a $1,500 closet to test that.

Read 30 Sep 2026.

**Brief description:**
A plug-in steam closet with no plumbing. Five-hanger model: hangers “move clothes through steam using 6 different motions.” Category page: the moving hanger “gently shakes garments dry.” It is built for refreshing worn clothes and drying delicates, not a 15 lb wet wash. QuickRefresh is listed as about 22 minutes (refresh, not a full wet load).

**Reflection:**
I like that motion and spacing are designed, not left to whoever is holding a wet towel. On a cheap rack the shirt sits on the same contact line for hours, which is the damp patch people poke. I do not want a motor shaking laundry in a small apartment for a whole dry cycle. The cheaper version of the same idea is rod shape and standoff so cloth never seals against a vent, which I can prototype without a Styler.

**Link / source:**
Costway item 42367819, $59.99, read 30 Sep 2026.
https://www.costway.com/100w-electric-towel-warmer-drying-rack.html
Seller claims: 100W, five rods, 131°F / 55°C, floor stand or wall mount.
Dry:Soon Deluxe: 300W heated aluminium bars, no fan, £199.99 and 6 to 12 hour times in Tech Advisor’s review (independent, one unit).

**Brief description:**
These make the bars themselves do the work. You hang clothes the same way as on a cheap rack. Heat reaches what touches a bar. Dry:Soon users and testers still report damp patches on parts that are not touching. Costway is sold in the US more like a bathroom towel warmer than as a whole-load laundry rack (five rods, 22 in wide).

**Reflection:**
I like the interaction: the user does nothing new, the rod is smarter. That is the opposite of asking someone to reconfigure a frame every load. I dislike that heat without moving air only dries the contact line, which is exactly the complaint I keep finding. My project claim, if I go this way, should be air delivery, not “another heated bar.”

## #6. Perforated air duct / even-flow pipe (plus Huff drying-bar patent)

[Place image / screenshot here]
Suggested screenshots:
1. DuctSox fabric duct / linear vent: https://www.ductsox.com/design
2. US 5,642,462 patent drawing (hollow tube with slots blowing onto fabric), already in my lab:
https://patents.google.com/patent/US5642462A/en
Related fabric-duct patent: https://patents.google.com/patent/US5782689A/en

**Link / source:**
DuctSox design: https://www.ductsox.com/design
US 5,782,689, fabric-faced air distribution device.
US 5,642,462, Huff, “Fabric article drying rack assembly… utilizing heated air flow,” granted 1997 (laundry-specific, not currently a product on a shelf).

**Brief description:**
HVAC and fabric ducts solve “air must leave evenly along a long pipe,” using open-area ratios, taper, slots vs orifices vs porous fabric. Huff already applied that to a drying bar: air through a tube, out slots onto the garment, taper for even flow, ribs so fabric does not seal the vents. Nothing I found on sale today pushes air out through laundry rods. NuBreeze and Foxydry blow into the space around the load instead.

**Reflection:**
I like that “could the bars move air?” has an engineering answer (hole area vs bore, taper, exit type) instead of a vibe. I dislike copying HVAC millwork or pretending we invented hollow bars. The interesting gap is close-range wet cloth on the holes, which ducts never deal with. That is the first bench test I actually want to run.

Say this first (about 30 seconds)

Lead with the question, not the product.

How can one drying system adapt to different laundry loads and living spaces, instead of requiring several different racks?

Then the two axes. Keep them in this order.

  • How it installs. Where the rack can live.
  • How it configures. What the laundry needs.

Then say the honest part out loud, early: this is very early ideation. The direction, the mechanisms, and even which kind of adaptability we chase may change.

The problem in one line

The space changes. The laundry changes. The rack usually doesn't.

Same locked rack, four different situations: small load, long pants, large towel load, different apartment. Most racks make the user adapt to the product.

Where the idea came from

Three precedents. Each one solves a piece. None combine them.

The opening we think we see: combining installation adaptability with configuration adaptability in one system.

What "adaptable" could mean, and what we cut

Four kinds. We are exploring two.

  • Installation (exploring). Floor, wall, door, tension, railing clamp.
  • Shape and geometry (exploring). Tall for pants. Narrow for a corner. Wider when there's room.
  • Capacity (not first). Add a section or a tier.
  • Attachments (not first). Hanger bar, clips, flat-dry shelf.

Point out that cutting two was deliberate. The frame's own form changes. It is not an accessory kit.

The direction, if he wants specifics

One core structure. Interchangeable installation modules. A reconfigurable drying frame.

  1. Adapt to the space. The same core could connect with floor legs, wall brackets, over-door support, a tension mount, or a railing clamp. First tests: floor plus wall. The rest is system logic, not the deliverable.
  2. Adapt to the laundry. The frame could get taller, shorter, narrower, wider, or reposition bars. Example: taller so pants don't touch the floor.

If he wants it concrete, use branch A from the deck: small apartment, limited floor space, wall mount, long pants and shirts, tall and narrow config, folds toward the wall afterward.

The one thing I most want his read on

Which is more valuable: adapting the rack to the space, adapting it to the laundry, or combining both?

Say plainly that we are not sure the combination is the strong move. It might be the thing that makes it too complicated.

Where I know it's soft (say it before he finds it)

  • No target user yet. We don't know who this is for.
  • No evidence yet that install adaptability or geometry change is actually wanted.
  • Weight and stability with wet clothes is unproven.
  • Reconfiguration might not be easy enough to be worth doing.
  • How much adaptability becomes too much complexity is an open question.

Questions for Chopra

  1. Space, laundry, or both? Which would you chase first?
  2. Is a standardized install connection the interesting part, or is it plumbing?
  3. Which installation modes are worth prototyping? Which geometry changes?
  4. Is this scope right for a capstone: one functional core, two installation configs, two or more meaningful geometries, plus user testing?
  5. What research would actually change our minds here, not just confirm us?
  6. We drafted four ways to show the user journey: steps, table, task flow, and situation branches. Which format communicates the idea best to a reviewer?
  7. Are we cutting capacity and attachments too early?

What I want to walk out with

  • A read on the space vs. laundry vs. both question.
  • A sense of whether the deliverable is too big, too small, or right.
  • One journey format to keep.
  • Any lead on a third teammate. Austin and I are open to one more person who likes prototyping, mechanisms, fabrication, or product design.

Assignment section, 30 September 2026. ATLAS 4000.

Research question for this pass

How can a drying system actively improve the process of air-drying clothes through airflow, materials, geometry, or integrated mechanisms, while fitting naturally into someone’s living space?

Did this change from last week?

This week, my focus has shifted from primarily looking at modular drying-rack configurations and renter-friendly installation methods to exploring what could make the drying system itself more effective and thoughtfully designed. After talking with Aidan, I am questioning whether users would actually want to spend time reconfiguring a rack for every load, so I want to investigate other ways the product could adapt or improve the experience without adding more work for the user.

I am especially interested in airflow, rod geometry, materials, garment spacing, and systems where the structure itself helps clothes dry. I am also trying not to constrain the project too early around cost. While an affordable product could still be a possible direction, my priority right now is finding the strongest and most interesting capstone opportunity, even if that eventually leads to a more premium or technologically complex product.

What the six new precedents are for

They are ordered as a story, starting from the passive rack already in the lab (Polder Mountain Lock and Brabantia WallFix, filed in previous passes) and ending at living with the object in a real room.

  • Active drying, the rack as the working part. heated-airer-plus adds a second, US-sold heated structure next to the Dry:Soon heated airer already filed, so the argument is not resting on one British product. The interaction model is the thing to steal: the rod does more, the user does nothing different.
  • Airflow built into an object. dyson-air-multiplier is here for the air path as product, hidden impeller in the base, and a 2024 parametric study saying slit thickness beats every other geometry variable. Industrial design and air-as-structure only. Dyson has never made a drying rack.
  • Air directed around each hanging garment. samsung-airdresser is a closed cabinet that, in Samsung’s own words, uses a “Jet Air system and Air Hangers” to “blow powerful jets of air upwards and downwards” and dries with “an upper JetAir and a lower HeatPump.” It is the industrial confirmation that evenness, not speed, is the airflow problem.
  • Garment interaction, motion and spacing. lg-styler moves the hanger through six different motions and describes a system that “gently shakes garments dry.” It proves that how the cloth sits and moves relative to the air is a designed variable, and that the mechanical version of that is the loud, complicated one.
  • Air through structure, kept even along a length. perforated-duct is the engineering file: fabric ducts, linear slot diffusers, perforated pipe and drip-irrigation laterals, with the hole-area ratio and the tapered bore as testable rules. It gives the 1997 Huff patent already in the lab an engineering context and turns it into prototype variables.
  • Enclosure, and living with it. drying-pod is the crude forced-air version that probably works and is certainly ugly, which isolates containment as the cheapest big idea in the pass. live-with-it is the seventh file, the Sheila Maid ceiling airer, an object people keep out on purpose and which does nothing at all. The capstone sits between those two.

On cost

Not researching affordability this pass. No price comparison, no bill of materials, no target retail. The prices recorded in the precedent files are there to locate the category, from a $59.99 heated towel rack to a $1,699 steam closet, not to pick a price point.

I can always value-engineer later. Right now the stronger question is what is the coolest, most useful drying system I could realistically design and prototype for capstone.

Sounding board with Aidan Chopra, 29 September 2026. Went in with install modularity plus reconfigurable geometry. Came out with two competing problems instead of one concept.

What he challenged

The whole pitch assumed reconfiguration is a feature. He asked whether anyone actually wants to do it. Not whether it is possible to build, whether a person would spend time on it every single load.

  • How often would someone really change the configuration?
  • Do different loads genuinely need substantially different setups, or do they need the same rack and slightly different draping?
  • Is modularity solving a real problem, or adding a new chore to a chore?

His frame for the goal: easy and passive. If the rack asks for a decision and two minutes of fiddling before you can hang a shirt, it has made laundry worse.

What he wanted me to look at instead of the object

The full laundry experience, not the rack in isolation. The wash finishes, and then what. Where do the wet clothes sit while you wait. How long does hanging actually take. What kinds of clothes are in the load. How do you arrange them and why. What is annoying. And then the long part nobody designs for: the rack sits in the room for a day or two, and you live around it.

The phrase worth keeping: how do you live without going into laundry mode. Can it stay out. Can friends come over. Does the room stop being your room.

The new direction: active airflow

Instead of reshaping the rack, make the rack help clothes dry faster. Not a fan sitting on the floor pointed at a pile of wet laundry. The structure itself distributes the air: hollow or perforated rods, air pushed through the frame, exiting through holes aimed at the garment that is hanging right there.

The positioning he sketched was premium and engineered rather than cheap and folding. Integrated airflow, shaped rods, quieter fans, chosen materials, purpose-built clips, geometry that exists because of the air path. His shorthand for the target: if Dyson made a drying rack.

A custom clothespin designed for that specific bar came up as the one attachment that might be worth keeping, because it would belong to the airflow system rather than being a generic accessory.

Experiments he named

  • Fan plus PVC plus drilled holes. Wet fabric on the tube, wet fabric on a normal bar, compare.
  • Variables: rod diameter, rod shape (round, square, flat, oval), material, hole pattern, fan strength, fabric type, airflow versus none.
  • Measures: how much faster, how loud, where the air actually exits, whether it reaches the far side of the garment.
  • Custom clips as a later variable, once there is a bar worth designing a clip for.

Next steps, in his order

  1. Observe. Document the next six loads. Hang normally. Photograph everything. Record what was in the load and how each item ended up where it did.
  2. Validate. Talk to more people, especially women and people who hang-dry a wider range of garments, plus apartments and dorms and habitual air-dryers. Find out whether reconfiguration is wanted at all.
  3. Experiment. Separately, build the airflow test. It does not depend on the answer to the reconfiguration question.
  4. Then choose. See which of the two problems produces the more interesting opportunity, and make that the capstone.

What this changes in the lab

Install plus geometry is no longer the locked concept. It is track one of two, and track one now starts with a question rather than a deliverable. The agenda file is updated to match: vault/research-agenda-drying-rack.md.

Nothing from the earlier passes gets deleted. Polder is still 40 lb. Command Large is still a 15 lb picture load. Those findings survive whichever track wins.

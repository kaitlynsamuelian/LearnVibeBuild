What it proves

A closed cabinet with a designed air path around each hanging garment is a shipping product at appliance prices. Samsung’s own wording on the page we read: the machine uses a “Jet Air system and Air Hangers” to “blow powerful jets of air upwards and downwards,” and it performs “low temperature drying using an upper JetAir and a lower HeatPump,” which “improve the internal circulation of air and dry clothes evenly.” Wrinkle care is described as “a combination of steam and air.”

Read that last quote again, because it is the sentence this whole research pass was looking for. A manufacturer with a real lab decided the way to dry hanging clothes evenly is air from above plus air from below plus a heat pump, and said evenness out loud as the goal rather than speed. Our agenda ticket A6 is built on the guess that evenness is the real user complaint. This is the closest thing to industrial confirmation we have.

The Air Hanger is the second finding, and it is the one worth chasing. The hanger is a hollow fitting the garment is loaded onto, and multiple reviews and Samsung-adjacent summaries describe air being driven through the hanger into the inside of the garment rather than only around the outside. If that is correct, someone has already shipped the air-through-the-structure idea at the hanger scale while leaving the rack scale open. Flagged: we read that through-the-hanger description in review and search-summary language, not on the official page we opened, so it needs verifying against the user manual before it is claimed in class.

What it does not do

It is not a drying rack and it does not pretend to be. Capacity is three jackets and three pairs of trousers, all on hangers. A real air-dry load in the six-loads protocol includes towels, socks, underwear, bras and knits laid flat, and none of that fits this machine or its hanger fittings.

It is a 89 kg piece of furniture that costs roughly as much as a small car repair, and the US variant is already marked discontinued at one retailer, which is worth noting as a market signal rather than a specification.

NOT FOUND: a published decibel rating, a wattage, or any stated drying time for a wet washed load. Samsung positions drying as gentle care for delicates, not as a replacement for a dryer, so there is no dry-time number to borrow even if we wanted one.

And it solves the appearance problem by becoming a cabinet. That is available to us only if we are willing to design a wardrobe, which is a different capstone.

Link to our concept

What is this expensive thing doing that a cheap folding rack is not? Three things. It encloses the volume so the air cannot leave before it has touched cloth. It attacks each garment from two directions, above and below, instead of from one fan on the floor. And it moves air through the thing the garment hangs on, which is exactly the geometry the Huff patent claimed for a bar and that nothing on sale does for a rack.

Steal the principle, not the price tag. The principle is that evenness is an airflow-geometry problem with a known industrial answer: enclose, then attack from two sides, then deliver through the support. A cheap rack does none of the three, and we can prototype all three without a heat pump. The Air Hanger in particular reframes our A9 clip ticket: the attachment worth designing may not be a clip on the bar, it may be the hanger itself as the last few centimetres of the air path.

US Patent 5,642,462 A, “Fabric article drying rack assembly mountable to a support assembly utilizing heated air flow.” Inventor Gary D. Huff. Filed 12 August 1994, granted 24 June 1997. https://patents.google.com/patent/US5642462A/en. Retrieved 2026-09-30. Not a product. Patent art, and expired.

“A rack assembly for drying and warming fabric articles which includes an elongated tube having an interior chamber housing an electrical dryer for warming the air and directing the air through the tube and over a plurality of elongated slots formed in the elongated tube to expel the air onto the fabric article.”

Related: US 7,552,545 B2, “Ducted drying rack for clothes dryer,” which ducts a rack into an existing tumble dryer.

What it proves

The exact idea from the Chopra meeting, hollow bars with air coming out of holes onto the garment, was described and patented three decades ago. We are not first. Saying so out loud is better than being told.

More usefully, it already solved two problems we were about to discover on the bench:

  • Even distribution. The internal cross-section tapers along the tube and the slots get shorter toward the far end, so air leaves along the whole length instead of all escaping near the inlet.
  • Fabric sealing the vents. Ribs between the slots hold the fabric off the openings so air can still get out and move downward.

Both are free hypotheses for the experiment. The second one identifies the most likely way our prototype fails.

What it does not do

A patent is not evidence that anything works or that anyone wants it. This one never became a product we can find on sale, and we do not know whether that was performance, cost, noise, or simply nobody trying. Do not read commercial failure into it and do not read validation into it either.

It also assumes heated air with an electrical dryer inside the tube, which is not the low-risk version we should build first. And it is a single bar mounted to a support, not a full rack with a load on it.

Link to our concept

This is the prior-art precedent. It reframes what we could claim as new: not the mechanism, but the air path geometry applied to a real mixed laundry load, with evidence about evenness rather than average speed, in a format a renter can own. It is also the cheapest source of test variables we have.

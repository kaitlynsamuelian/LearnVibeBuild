What it proves

The question “could the bars of the rack themselves move air” has a mature engineering answer, and the answer is a set of ratios rather than an invention.

One. Total hole area is the master variable. The rule as summarised from the forum thread: maldistribution is mostly a function of the area ratio, meaning total perforation area divided by pipe cross-sectional area, with little maldistribution, under 5%, at an area ratio below 0.5, and significant maldistribution, over 10%, above 1. In plain terms, if the holes add up to more than the bore, the air all leaves near the inlet. Flagged as forum guidance, but it is a bench-testable number and it is the first thing to check on our prototype.

Two. Open area is small in practice. The fabric diffuser patent gives effective open areas “preferably 10%” for induction diffusers and “between about 5% and 25%,” and fabric porosity of “5 to 40 scfm per foot squared at 0.5 inch water gauge pressure drop.” Those are officially filed numbers from a real air-distribution company, and 5% to 25% open area is far less hole than a first-time maker would drill.

Three. The whole length is the diffuser. DuctSox’s design argument is that “the entire DuctSox system functions as a diffuser delivering both air distribution and dispersion through the full run,” achieved by combining “air-porous fabrics, linear vents, nozzles and orifices,” against metal systems that “discharge air through side mounted metal diffusers, with the air directed to specific zones” and end up “often causing drafting and hot or cold spots.” Hot and cold spots in a warehouse are damp patches on a shirt. Same failure, same cause.

Four. Exit type trades throw against gentleness. From the same page: linear vents give “Quiet, low-velocity airflow,” orifices are a “High-throw, non-porous solution,” fixed nozzles deliver “jet-like performance for focused airflow,” and adjustable nozzles give “360° directional control” and are “Great for fine-tuning coverage and avoiding condensation zones.” That last phrase matters for us, because condensation is the named hazard of an unheated prototype in agenda ticket A8.

Five. Two other industries solve it by geometry and by valve. Varying the duct cross-section along its length is a published approach to uniform distribution, which is precisely the tapered bore Huff claimed for a drying bar in 1994. Drip irrigation instead puts a flexing diaphragm in every emitter so each one self-corrects, with a design target of no more than about 10% variation between the highest and lowest emitter over roughly a 10 to 30 psi range. Taper the pipe, or make every hole smart. One of those is available to a student with a hacksaw.

What it does not do

None of this is about wet cloth. Every source above distributes air into open space. Not one accounts for a saturated towel pressed against the discharge opening, which is the failure mode agenda ticket A4 exists to test and the reason the Huff patent put ribs between its slots.

The scales are also wrong in our favour and against us at once. HVAC ducts run at pressures, lengths and volumes a small fan cannot reach, so their numbers are ratios to borrow, not values to copy. A 12 ft slot diffuser is not a 30 in drying bar, and the Titus throw multipliers by section length, which we have only as a search summary, are about room throw rather than about a shirt 2 in away.

NOT FOUND anywhere in this material: any data on airflow through fabric in contact with the discharge, any guidance for discharge onto a surface at close range, and any acoustic figure for a small perforated tube.

Finally, steal manifold design and not HVAC millwork. Nothing here justifies a diffuser aesthetic, a plenum, or the vocabulary of building services in a capstone about laundry.

Link to our concept

What are these engineered things doing that a cheap folding rack is not? They treat “the air must leave evenly along a length” as a solved design problem with governing ratios. A cheap rack has no length to distribute along, because it has no air, and a desk fan pointed at the rack is the zoned metal-diffuser approach that DuctSox says causes hot and cold spots.

Steal the principle, not the price tag. This file converts into three concrete prototype rules for experiments/airflow-experiment.html:

  • Keep total hole area under about half the tube bore. Start well under the 0.5 area ratio, and note that the filed fabric-diffuser numbers imply 5% to 25% open area rather than a tube full of holes. Drill fewer holes than instinct says.
  • Choose between taper and self-correction. Huff tapered the bore and shortened the slots toward the far end. Irrigation instead compensates at each outlet. Test the taper, because it is cuttable, and note the compensating-emitter idea as the version a manufacturer would do.
  • Pick an exit type on purpose, and for our load. Slot, orifice or porous surface are three different products. Dyson’s patent and the 2024 parametric study both say the slot is where the performance is, see precedents/dyson-air-multiplier.html, and the DuctSox language says low-velocity linear vents are the quiet option. Quiet matters for A7.

Our defensible new ground stays the same and gets sharper: not the mechanism, which is a century of duct engineering and a 1997 patent, but the air path designed for a wet mixed load at close range, measured for evenness. Nobody in these four industries has that data, because none of them is drying laundry.

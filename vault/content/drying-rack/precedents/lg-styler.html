Official wording, quoted from those pages. Five-hanger model: “Dynamic MovingHanger™,” and “5 hangers move clothes through steam using 6 different motions for various garment types and cycles,” with “Dual TrueSteam® Technology” and “Dual Inverter HeatPump™ helps to avoid damage while refreshing clothes and drying delicates.” Three-hanger model: “Moving hangers effectively move clothes through the circulating hot steam, up to 3 items,” listed at $1,699.00 from $2,099.00, with a “Plug & Go” install and no plumbing. Category page: “Its moving hanger system gently shakes garments dry, releasing wrinkles and restoring shape.” QuickRefresh is given as “about 22 minutes.”

Flagged: a search summary of LG material says “At roughly 40 dB, this quiet steamer for clothes is ideal for bedrooms, closets, and small spaces.” We did not see that sentence on the pages we opened. NOT FOUND on the pages we read: movements per minute, cabinet dimensions, wattage, and any drying time for a wet load.

What it proves

Garment motion is a designed variable, not an accident. LG did not simply increase airflow. It moves the hanger, and it distinguishes six different motions by garment type and cycle. That is a manufacturer saying that how the cloth moves relative to the air matters enough to build a mechanism and a menu for it.

The category-page verb is the one to keep: the system “gently shakes garments dry.” Motion is being sold as part of drying, not only as wrinkle removal.

It also proves that spacing gets designed once you commit to hangers. Three items, or five, each on its own fitting, each with clearance around it. Compare that with a rack, where the user decides spacing in ten seconds while holding a wet towel, and where the failure mode filed in online/reconfiguration-skepticism.html is that items shield each other and dry unevenly.

And the install note is a genuine precedent for us. Both models are plug-in with no plumbing, which is the same renter-safe instinct the earlier install research pass landed on.

What it does not do

It holds three to five garments. It is a wardrobe-sized cabinet at $1,699 for the small one. It is built for refreshing worn clothes and drying delicates, not for a 15 lb wet wash, and LG never claims otherwise.

The moving hanger is also the noisiest and most failure-prone way to get the same result. A review comparison we read in search summaries puts it plainly: the LG shakes the hanger rod mechanically while the Samsung blows air through the hanger, and the mechanical one is the louder of the two. That is a paraphrase, not a measurement, but it points at our A7 ticket. A motor that shakes a wet load for hours in a studio apartment is a liveability problem and a mechanism that can jam.

NOT FOUND: any published measurement of what the motion contributes to drying rate on its own, separated from steam and the heat pump. So we cannot claim motion helps by any specific amount.

Link to our concept

What is this expensive thing doing that a cheap folding rack is not? It is refusing to let the garment sit still in one position for the whole cycle. On a cheap rack the shirt touches the rail in exactly the same place for ten hours, and that contact line is the damp patch people come back and poke at. LG’s answer is to move the garment. Huff’s answer, already in the lab, was to hold the fabric off the vent with ribs. Samsung’s answer is to blow through the hanger. Three different companies attacking the same contact problem.

Steal the principle, not the price tag. The principle is that the contact surface is the design problem, and motion is one of at least three legitimate ways at it. The cheap version of motion is not a motor: it is rod cross-section and standoff, so the garment never has a long flat contact line in the first place. That is an A5 variable we can test with a saw and a scale, and it is the honest low-cost competitor to a shaking mechanism. If we ever do want motion, the interesting question for capstone is whether a puff of air can move a garment without a motor, which is a question nobody on this shelf has answered.

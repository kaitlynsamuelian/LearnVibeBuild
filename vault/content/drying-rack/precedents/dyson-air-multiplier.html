Dyson Air Multiplier, the bladeless fan family (AM01 onward, currently sold as Dyson Cool). Not a drying rack, not a laundry product. Filed here as industrial design and air-path engineering. Primary source we could actually open: US Patent 8,092,166 B2, “Fan,” inventors Frederic Nicolas and Kevin John Simmonds, assignee Dyson Technology Ltd, filed 20 November 2009, granted 10 January 2012, https://patents.google.com/patent/US8092166B2/en. What it proves

The patent is the useful part, because it describes the object as an air path rather than as a fan with a cover on it. In its own words the assembly is “a nozzle mounted on a base housing a device for creating an air flow through the nozzle,” with “a Coanda surface located adjacent the mouth and over which the mouth is arranged to direct the air flow.” The impeller “driven by a motor” is “preferably located in a base of the fan assembly.” The moving part is hidden low and the visible part is the geometry that shapes the air.

Two numbers from the patent are directly relevant to a rack bar. The nozzle is “annular or substantially annular,” because “by providing an annular nozzle the fan can potentially reach a broad area.” And the mouth outlet spacing is given “in the range from 0.5 mm to 10 mm.” That is a slot, not a hole, and it is small.

The 2024 parametric study says the slot is the variable that matters most. It reports that “the discharge ratio increases threefold for all MFRi when the slit thickness is reduced from 1.5 mm to 0.25 mm,” and that “a discharge ratio greater than 10 can be achieved with combinations of small slit thickness and radius.” Radius behaved non-linearly and mass flow rate changed the shape of the curve, but slit thickness dominated. This is the first sourced reason to believe that our A5 rod test should start with exit geometry rather than with a bigger fan.

The industrial design lesson is separate and just as important. It is a calm single form, it does not read as an appliance, and nothing about it says machine. That is the standard for something that has to stand in a living room for six hours.

What it does not do

It does not dry clothes, and Dyson has never made a drying rack or an airer, which the lab already established in agenda ticket A10. Anything said in class has to stop at design logic.

The commercial claims we can find do not agree with each other. A search summary of the Dyson Cool CF1 page gives 30 watts at maximum fan speed, 97 gallons of airflow per second, and amplification of surrounding air “up to 13 times.” A search summary of the Dyson Cool technology page gives an impeller forcing “up to 7 gallons of air per second into the loop,” entrainment “amplifying the initial airflow by as much as 15 times,” and a ramp “angled at precisely 16°.” Thirteen times and fifteen times are not the same claim. Until someone opens those pages in a browser, treat all six of those figures as unverified marketing. The search summary that quotes the patent family as amplifying “by at least five times, more preferably by at least ten times” is also a paraphrase we read in a result list, not on the page.

The bigger trap is the motor. Dyson’s advantage is a brushless motor and a decade of flow simulation. We cannot build a motor, and a capstone that competes on air volume loses.

Link to our concept

What is this expensive thing doing that a cheap folding rack is not? It is treating the air path as the product. A $25 rack has no air path at all, and the $60 fan sitting next to it has an air path that ends at the fan face. Dyson moved the machinery into a base, made the aperture a narrow annular slot, and let the surrounding room air do most of the work through entrainment. All three of those are free to copy in a hollow drying bar: put the fan in the foot, make the exit a slot with a chosen width, and let entrainment carry room air up through the garment rather than trying to blow a wet shirt dry with raw volume.

Steal the principle, not the price tag. The specific thing to steal is that slot width beat everything else in the parametric study, which turns into a concrete bench variable: run the A5 rod test with slot widths in the single millimetres before touching fan size. The 0.5 mm to 10 mm range in the patent is a defensible starting bracket, and we get to say where it came from.

Dry:Soon Deluxe 3-Tier Heated Clothes Airer, sold by Lakeland in the UK, product code 24909. Electrically heated aluminium bars, no fan. Seller claims 21 metres of drying space across 36 heated bars, up to 15kg of washing, 70 x 74 x 132.5cm, folds to 8cm deep, 3-year guarantee. Lakeland’s own running-cost claim: “Costs just 8p to run vs 65p for a standard tumble dryer.” Tech Advisor’s review lists it at 300W and £199.99. Retrieved 2026-09-30.

What it proves

People will pay roughly £200 for a drying rack if it dries faster. This is the same folding-floor-rack format we have been treating as a commodity, at eight times the price, selling on performance alone. The category is not stuck at $25 as long as the rack actually does something.

It also proves the market accepts a rack that plugs in and stays plugged in for hours. That removes one objection to the airflow direction before we raise it.

What it does not do

It does not move air. It heats bars, and the heat only reaches what is touching a bar. Tech Advisor’s reviewer, using it without a cover, recorded 6 hours for shirts and delicates, 10 hours for bulky items and 12 hours for bedding, and wrote that “even after catching some Zzzs, parts of my clothing were still damp.” Users on Mumsnet say the same thing more bluntly: “parts not touching the bars just don’t get dry.”

The fix the product expects is a separate £45 cover, which trades the appearance problem for a worse one. A £200 rack with a sheet thrown over it is not a design answer to living in laundry mode.

And it is still a full-width floor object standing in the room for most of a day.

Link to our concept

This is the “drying faster is worth money” precedent and the “heat alone is not enough” precedent at the same time. It is the strongest commercial argument for the airflow track and the clearest description of the gap: heat without air delivery leaves exactly the surfaces users complain about. Do not position against it on price. Position against it on whether the air ever reaches the cloth.

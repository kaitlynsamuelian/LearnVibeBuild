Ten questions, written 2026-09-30. Closes ticket U1. Sit-down or video, 20 to 30 minutes. The order matters more than the wording: the whole laundry sequence first, then living in the space, then layout, then speed. Layout comes ninth on purpose. If you ask about reconfiguring early you will get a polite yes and learn nothing.

Before you start

  • Say what this is: a senior capstone about home laundry, no product to sell, nothing they say will offend you.
  • Ask to record. If they say no, take notes and write them up within the hour.
  • Ask them to have their rack in the room, or a photo of it, if that is easy.
  • Do not describe the concept. Not at the start, not in the middle. If they ask what you are building, say you are still figuring out what the problem is, which is true.

The questions

1. Walk me through the last time you did laundry. Start from the machine finishing.

Let this run long. Do not steer. Everything else in the script is a follow-up to something in here.

Listen for: how soon they got to it, what they were doing instead, whether the load got split.

2. Where did the wet clothes sit before they went up?

Listen for: basket, floor, left in the drum, on a bed. How long. Whether that gap bothers them.

3. Tell me about hanging that load. What went up first?

Follow-ups: what went where, and why that spot. Did anything not fit. Did anything end up somewhere that is not the rack.

Listen for: a repeated personal system versus improvising. Whether the system is about the garment or about the rack.

4. Which things are the most annoying to hang?

Follow-up: what makes them annoying. Small, heavy, shapeless, needs a clip, nowhere to put it.

Listen for: item count versus item awkwardness. Socks and bras versus jeans and towels.

5. What kinds of clothes do you hang, and what would you never hang?

Follow-up: what goes in the dryer instead, and why. Where the line is.

Listen for: whether air-drying is a fallback or a deliberate care choice for specific garments.

6. Once it is all up, what happens in that room for the next day or two?

Follow-ups: where the rack is standing. Do you walk around it. Does anyone else in the space say anything about it. Has anyone come over while it was up.

Listen for: the words they use about how it looks. Do not supply the word “eyesore.”

7. When does it go away, and who puts it away?

Follow-up: is it away right now. Where does it live when it is away. Has it ever just stayed out for a week.

Listen for: whether folding away is a real behaviour at all. Our earlier concept assumed it was the core interaction.

8. Is there a part of this you have tried to fix or work around?

Follow-up: what did you try, did it work, are you still doing it.

Listen for: fans, open windows, radiators, a second rack, dehumidifiers, moving the rack to a warmer room. These are unprompted votes for speed.

9. Has there ever been a moment where you wished the rack itself were shaped differently?

This is the layout question, and it is deliberately asked as a memory, not as a preference. If they say no, accept it and move on. Do not rescue the idea.

Follow-ups only if they say yes: what were you holding at the time. What would you have wanted it to do. Would you have done that every load, or was that a one-off.

Listen for: whether the wish is about shape, about more space, or about something not touching something else.

10. If laundry dried twice as fast, what would actually change for you?

Ask this last and only after nine. It is leading, so it is the final question, and its job is to test whether speed is worth anything to them rather than to discover the idea.

Follow-ups: would you still need the rack to be smaller. Would you pay for that. Would you tolerate a fan running in the room. What is too loud.

Things not to say

  • “Modular.” “Reconfigurable.” “Adaptable.” “System.”
  • “Would you like it if…” Anything phrased that way gets a yes.
  • Any description of the rack we mocked up. They cannot un-see it.

Who to ask

See ticket U2 in the agenda. Priority is women and anyone who hang-dries a wide range of garments, plus dorm and apartment residents and people who air-dry as a routine rather than an emergency. Five conversations before locking either track.
